## Audience

**Jordan:** a college student who uses Instagram, Snapchat, Google, and AI chatbots almost every day. She likes the convenience and knows these services collect information. She hasn't looked closely at how privacy has changed over time.

**Working need:** Jordan needs a short explanation, backed by sources, of how surveillance grew during the Iraq War era. That history can help her understand the privacy questions behind technology she already uses.

**0:00–0:30 — What I made**

“My website is called How the Iraq War Affected Digital Privacy Rights. It looks at the PATRIOT Act, government access to phone records, surveillance across borders, and biometric identification in Iraq. The timeline gives readers a way to follow what happened. It also makes the dates clear, since the Iraq War and the wider response to 9/11 aren't the same thing.”

**0:30–1:10 — Sources and adaptation**

“Two of my primary sources are the USA PATRIOT Act (2001) and a government report on military biometrics (U.S. Government Accountability Office, 2008b). I also use Stefka Hristova’s peer-reviewed article about biometrics in Iraq (Hristova, 2014). She looks at how identifying people became a way to judge whether they were a security threat. The site uses shorter explanations, but keeps that difference between knowing someone's identity and making a judgment about them (Hristova, 2014, pp. 516–517). It also explains the difference between phone metadata and what someone actually says in a call (Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board, 2014b, pp. 21–22).”

Show the original law, GAO report, and Hristova link. Point out that the PATRIOT Act passed before the 2003 invasion. Explain that government documents can't tell the whole story of how Iraqis experienced surveillance.

**1:10–1:45 — Design and audience**

“The site is written for Jordan, a student who uses technology every day and hasn't spent much time thinking about the history of privacy. The headings are straightforward, the explanations are short, and the sources are linked so she can check them. That connects to D’Ignazio and Klein’s idea of considering context (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020, Chapter 6). Each example explains who collected the information, why they collected it, and what they could do with it.”

## Proposed safety checks

Use your team's charter to guide the final check. Keep the dates straight and make clear where a claim comes from. Explain metadata separately from call contents. Check that the site doesn't blame every post-9/11 law on the Iraq War or treat a biometric match as proof of guilt. Keep personal records out, don't invent testimony, and explain how AI helped. Ask before quoting someone's feedback.

You can record replies, reposts, likes, and questions by hand. There's no need to add tracking for the class project. Feedback from a few volunteers can help you improve the site, though it won't tell you how everyone would respond.
